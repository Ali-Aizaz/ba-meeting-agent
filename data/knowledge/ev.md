# EV

An EV (battery electric vehicle, or BEV) is an electric vehicle driven by one or more electric motors. The energy is stored in a high-voltage traction battery pack (measured in kWh) that is charged from an external supply. An inverter converts DC battery power to AC for the traction motor, and a battery management system (BMS) monitors cell voltage, temperature, and state of charge. There is no fuel tank and no exhaust pipe.

## Charging

Home and workplace charging (Level 1 and Level 2) uses an AC supply and the vehicle's onboard charger, which is slower. A public DC fast charger bypasses the onboard charger to deliver DC power directly to the battery pack and is much quicker.

## Battery chemistry

Modern EV battery packs commonly use lithium iron phosphate (LFP) cells for lower cost, thermal stability, and long cycle life, or nickel manganese cobalt (NMC) cells for higher energy density and cold-weather range.

## 400V vs 800V architecture and heat pumps

High-voltage EV architectures use 400V or 800V battery packs. Higher pack voltage (800V) supports faster DC fast charging at lower current, cutting resistive heat and copper cable mass. Many modern EVs also use a cabin heat pump instead of purely resistive heaters to scavenge waste thermal energy from the motor and inverter, preserving winter range.

## WLTP vs EPA range

EPA and WLTP are standardized laboratory test cycles for estimating electric vehicle range and efficiency. WLTP (used in Europe and many global markets) tends to yield higher range estimates than the stricter US EPA multi-cycle procedure, especially for highway speeds.

## Regenerative braking and one-pedal driving

In an EV, regenerative braking runs the traction motor in reverse as a generator during deceleration, recovering kinetic energy back into the battery pack and enabling one-pedal driving in stop-and-go traffic.
